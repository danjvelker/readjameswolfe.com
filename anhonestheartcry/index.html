Don't Admonish an Honest Heart Cry

By James Wolfe  ·  November 14, 2026  ·  8 min read

I have no idea what I was made for.

Most of the people in my life have families, spouses and children. Some of them have built ministries and achieved great things in service of others. Several of my closest friends serve as elders or deacons in our church, and it feels to me like their lives are always full of ways to serve others, while for me…

Nothing I do ever feels like enough.

I try to live the kind of Christian life that I read about. I devote myself to seeking joy in the Lord, to having steadfast rhythms of prayer and thanksgiving, to reading scripture and enjoying fellowship with others. I’m always seeking to go deeper in my relationship with my God and Savior. I do all of these things, not out of a desire to earn my salvation, but out of genuine gratitude that Christ has saved me, and yet, still…

Most days I feel totally empty.

“What am I even good for?” That question echoes through my prayer life a lot. “Is there even a single meaningful part of my life?” I doubt I’m the only one who has ever felt like that. “Was I even made for a purpose, or do I just exist for nothing?” Most people could admit that these kinds of questions have troubled them at least once in their lives; but for many of us, trapped in a world drained of meaning and wonder, these questions are far more familiar, even daily companions.

We should understand that those questions reflect wrong thinking, and ultimately deserve correction. There are so many places where Scripture would counter the assumptions behind each of those questions. And yet I’m thankful that when I find these same kinds of thoughts expressed in Psalm 102 — where the psalmist feels useless, forgotten, unsettled, and distressed — rather than intellectually disbar each one, he nurtures them before the Lord and allows them to breathe:

Hear my prayer, O LORD; let my cry come to you! Do not hide your face from me in the day of my distress! Incline your ear to me; answer me speedily in the day when I call! For my days pass away like smoke, and my bones burn like a furnace. My heart is struck down like grass and has withered; I forget to eat my bread. Because of my loud groaning my bones cling to my flesh.

Psalm 102:1–5 ESV

In his cry of frustration, he doesn’t rush to admonish himself into a solution. He allows the starvation of his heart to bring him to a place of greater need and desire; and by doing so, he comes to express something true and beautiful about God that heals his heart. So with those words in mind, I want to point to three things we can learn from how this unnamed psalmist expresses his frustration.

Scripture validates our expressed frustration.

Too often, frustration gets categorized as a failure of belief instead of the honest expression of our hearts. We suspect that we might not be dealing with these negative emotions if only we were better believers with stronger faith. But these complaints are listed as part of God’s holy word.

By including Psalm 102 as part of Scripture, God reveals to us that he not only allows our frustration but encourages us to articulate it to him. He isn’t troubled by our troubles, nor frustrated by our frustrations. Like a good father, our Father in heaven encourages us to express – in raw and honest terms – all the outpouring of our hearts. Even the messy parts.

To be clear, righteous frustration is distinct from griping, complaining, or whining – those things are neither healthy nor helpful; but a prayer of pain that turns into praise is something that brings glory to God and healing to us. Psalm 102 is not griping, or whining, but unearthing. Like flipping over rocks to see what kinds of creatures are hidden beneath in the cold, damp earth, the psalmist is flipping over the stones of negative feeling in his heart to discover what lies beneath the feelings. He isn’t creating distress, but exposing it; and we ought to be encouraged to do the same.

Not only are we permitted to express the honest distress we experience, but God anticipates that we will do so, and creates room for us to share our hearts with him.

There is real truth in our misery and our weakness.

When the psalmist unearths his negative feelings, he isn’t completely wrong on the facts. It is likely that God was silent in that moment; the writer likely was impotent and hindered in his goals; depression and weariness were likely overwhelming. In other words: things probably were that tough, and what he describes was actually his reality while writing this psalm. There’s nothing to indicate that this writer was simply making things up, being overly sensitive, or just exaggerating his pain. Things really were difficult for him.

As long as we live in this broken world, joy and grief will be mingled. We do no service to God or to the truth when we try to pretend as though our misery isn’t all that miserable and in our weakness we aren’t really all that weak. It is, and we are.

Yet we have hope.

The real truth in our frustration is that our God is bigger. Yes, things really are that bad; and yes, God really is that good. He’s good enough to make all our sadness worth it. He’s great enough to pull us out of the worst sin. He’s kind enough to turn every bad thing to good in the end. But all of those things are still real, and when we fail to articulate them, we fail to articulate how good our Savior is for rescuing us from them. By trying to hide our misery, all we end up really doing is hiding how much we depend on salvation. We can – and should – express our hearts, especially in our distress.

It isn’t wrong to look honestly at the things that make us sad. In the end, no matter how bleak our situation, we can end with joy knowing that our great God is even greater.

Pain expressed in faith gives real glory to God.

Do you realize that what most frustrates you can become your greatest offering to God?

Those things which most frustrate us are likely some of the things that we hold dearest. If they weren’t important, they wouldn’t cause us so much distress when they didn’t go our way; but the nearness and preciousness is what gives them so much power over our hearts.

Will you entrust your most precious frustration to God?

This writer could have turned to any number of outlets with his unsettled heart. Calvin called our hearts relentless idol factories, and Augustine said that we will always be restless until we find our rest in God. Yet we turn to so many other comforts first. Sex, love, relaxation, validation, fast food, wealth – in other words, stuff. How often do you entrust your frustrations to these things? And how safe a guardian do they tend to be? But there’s a better option.

Take your heart. Press it into His hands – trust him to hold it safe, safer than you ever could. If it is good, then he will value it of far greater and higher worth than you ever could; and if it is deadly then he will dispose of it far quicker than you ever would have without him. Nothing that is ultimately good will be put to waste, and nothing that is ultimately evil will live a nanosecond longer than it must. So give your heart to God. Give your frustrated tears and stomach-churning distress to God.

He is glorified when you trust him with your treasures.

I still don’t really know what I was made for. But I can do what Psalm 102 does, which is to ultimately address despair by turning and looking to God:

But you, O LORD, are enthroned forever; you are remembered throughout all generations. You will arise and have pity on Zion; it is the time to favor her; the appointed time has come… For the LORD builds up Zion; he appears in his glory; he regards the prayer of the destitute and does not despise their prayer.

Psalm 102:12-13;16-17 ESV

I don’t think it’s wrong to feel the way that I do. There are ways I need to address my negative thoughts and feelings, taking them captive and submitting them to the truth of God’s word. But there are also ways that those feelings of sadness, anxiety and distress will ultimately point me to God in a far greater capacity than just shutting my eyes and plastering over them by pretending things are better than they are.

I’ll repeat that: the things that most frustrate you are probably the things that will bring you closest to God.

I ultimately feel a strange sense of peace and rightness even when those kinds of questions loom over me. That initial adverb – “ultimately” – is important, because in the immediate, the kind of distress that comes from questions of my meaning, value, and purpose is awfully uncomfortable. But Christians take the long view every time. If all we have is the immediate, then we really should scratch and scrabble for any momentary glimmer of happiness that we can get. But because we do have the ultimate ahead of us, we can give our hearts up to the God who will hold them more safely than we ever could.

Because of Jesus, my distress over long-term singleness is a speck on an eternal tapestry of glory. The unsettled feeling I sometimes get as a single man in a mostly-married church is light and momentary. My Lord is enthroned forever, and he hears my prayers. Maybe he’ll hear my distress and answer it with a spouse. Maybe he won’t, and I’ll have all of eternity to learn why his way was so much better.

So it’s fine to be sad. It’s OK to be unsettled, discouraged, and weary. Things are still going according to plan. Give God your distress. His holy Word validates many more of your feelings than you would expect. The Bible anticipates suffering, but it doesn’t leave it unanswered. Ultimately, God holds the answers to our questions. Sometimes he gives us those answers. Sometimes he doesn’t. But he never leaves us empty-handed.

When he doesn’t give us answers, he always gives us something better anyway.

He gives us Himself.
